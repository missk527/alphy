Notes

1. Refraction and Snell's law

Measure incidence and refraction angles from the normal, not the surface. A ray bending away from the normal enters a medium with a lower refractive index.

nA sin θA = nB sin θB

If θB > θA, then nA/nB = sin θB/sin θA > 1, so nA > nB.

Worked example: an angle of 43° to the surface gives an incidence angle of 90° − 43° = 47°. For nA = 1.62 and nB = 1.35, θB = sin−1(1.62 sin 47° / 1.35) = 61.4°.

2. Critical angle and total internal reflection

The critical angle is the incidence angle in the higher-index medium when the refracted ray is at 90° to the normal. Total internal reflection requires travel towards a lower-index medium and an incidence angle greater than the critical angle.

sin c = nlower / nhigher
  • For material B (n = 1.35) to air (n ≈ 1), c = sin−1(1/1.35) = 47.8° ≈ 48°.
  • In the speech's geometry: 90° − 61.4° = 28.6°; 180° − 137° − 28.6° = 14.4°; incidence angle = 90° − 14.4° = 75.6°.
  • Since 75.6° > 47.8°, total internal reflection occurs. Draw the reflected ray with its angle to the normal equal to the incidence angle.

3. Coherence, superposition and interference

Coherent sources have the same frequency and a constant phase difference. They need not be in phase. Two loudspeakers driven by the same signal generator can maintain this stable phase relationship.

By the principle of superposition, resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

  • Constructive interference: waves arrive in phase, giving increased amplitude.
  • Destructive interference: waves arrive in antiphase, giving reduced amplitude. Complete cancellation requires equal amplitudes at the observation point.
  • For in-phase sources, maxima occur at path differences mλ; minima occur at (m + ½)λ, where m is an integer.
  • Moving across the pattern changes the path difference, producing alternating loud and quiet regions. The central maximum A and the next maximum B have a minimum between them.

4. Calculating path difference and wave speed

Let speakers P and Q be 0.30 m apart with midpoint O. OA = 2.25 m is perpendicular to PQ. From A, move 0.95 m parallel to PQ towards P to reach B.

  • PB = √[2.25² + (0.95 − 0.15)²] = 2.388 m.
  • QB = √[2.25² + (0.95 + 0.15)²] = 2.504 m.
  • Path difference = QB − PB = 0.11651 m using unrounded lengths. Subtracting 2.50 − 2.39 gives the speech's rounded value of 0.11 m.
  • If B is the first maximum after A, its path difference equals one wavelength.
v = fλ

Using the speech's rounded λ = 0.11 m and f = 2960 Hz gives v = 325.6 m s−1. Keeping the full geometric precision instead gives about 345 m s−1. Keep intermediate values unrounded whenever possible.

5. Changing frequency at a fixed position

At constant speed, decreasing frequency increases wavelength. The geometric path difference stays fixed when the speakers and listener do not move; it is the phase difference that changes.

Δφ = 2π Δr / λ

This is the propagation phase difference; include any initial source phase difference when needed. Lower frequency spreads the interference maxima farther apart. At a particular point, the amplitude increases only if the waves move closer to being in phase; it does not necessarily increase for every decrease in frequency.

6. Stationary waves and harmonics

An ideal stationary wave forms when equal-amplitude waves of the same frequency and wavelength travel at the same speed in opposite directions and superpose.

  • Nodes: zero amplitude. Antinodes: maximum amplitude.
  • Particles within one loop oscillate in phase; particles in adjacent loops oscillate in antiphase, at the same frequency. Equal amplitudes require equivalent positions relative to the nodes.
  • The second harmonic of a string fixed at both ends has two loops. Adjacent nodes are λ/2 apart.
  • If a 0.55 m interval contains two wavelengths, λ = 0.275 m. At 250 Hz, v = 68.75 m s−1 ≈ 69 m s−1.
  • When the component displacements cancel everywhere, the string is momentarily straight. Antinode positions still exist because amplitude describes motion over time.
f1 = (1/2L) √(T/μ)     fn = nf1

For a string fixed at both ends, L is the vibrating length, T the tension, and μ the mass per unit length. Multiplying μ by nine makes the fundamental frequency one-third of its original value. If the driving frequency remains at the old fundamental, the new string resonates at its third harmonic.

Quiz: 40 Short Questions

Review refraction, total internal reflection, sound interference, path difference, and stationary waves.

1. State Snell's law.

Show answer

n1 sin(theta1) = n2 sin(theta2).

2. From which line are angles of incidence and refraction measured?

Show answer

The normal, perpendicular to the boundary.

3. A ray bends away from the normal as it travels from A to B. Which medium has the greater refractive index?

Show answer

Medium A: nA > nB.

4. Explain your answer to Question 3 using Snell's law.

Show answer

thetaB > thetaA, so sin(thetaB)/sin(thetaA) > 1. This ratio equals nA/nB; hence nA > nB.

5. A ray makes an angle of 43 degrees with a boundary. Find its angle to the normal.

Show answer

90 - 43 = 47 degrees.

6. Light travels from nA = 1.62 to nB = 1.35 at an incidence angle of 47 degrees. Calculate the refraction angle.

Show answer

thetaB = arcsin(1.62 sin(47 degrees)/1.35) = 61.4 degrees.

7. Define the critical angle.

Show answer

The angle of incidence in the higher-index medium for which the refracted ray travels along the boundary (refraction angle 90 degrees).

8. State the two conditions for total internal reflection.

Show answer

Light travels towards a lower-index medium, and its incidence angle exceeds the critical angle.

9. Write the critical-angle equation for light travelling from n1 to n2, where n1 > n2.

Show answer

sin(c) = n2/n1.

10. Calculate the critical angle for light travelling from a material of refractive index 1.35 into air (n = 1.00).

Show answer

c = arcsin(1/1.35) = 47.8 degrees, approximately 48 degrees.

11. A triangle has angles of 137 degrees and 28.6 degrees. Calculate its third angle.

Show answer

180 - 137 - 28.6 = 14.4 degrees.

12. A ray meets a boundary at 14.4 degrees to the surface. Calculate its incidence angle.

Show answer

90 - 14.4 = 75.6 degrees.

13. Light inside n = 1.35 reaches an air boundary at 75.6 degrees to the normal. What happens, and why?

Show answer

Total internal reflection occurs because 75.6 degrees exceeds the critical angle of 47.8 degrees.

14. If the incidence angle is 75.6 degrees and total internal reflection occurs, what is the reflection angle?

Show answer

75.6 degrees, measured from the normal.

15. What are coherent sources?

Show answer

Sources with the same frequency and a constant phase difference.

16. Why can two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator act as coherent sources?

Show answer

The common signal drives both at the same frequency with a stable phase relationship.

17. Must coherent sources be in phase?

Show answer

No. Their phase difference must be constant, but it need not be zero.

18. State the principle of superposition.

Show answer

Where waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of their individual displacements.

19. What phase relationship produces constructive interference?

Show answer

The waves arrive in phase (phase difference zero or a whole number of 2 pi radians).

20. What phase relationship produces destructive interference?

Show answer

The waves arrive in antiphase: a phase difference of pi radians, modulo 2 pi.

21. For in-phase sources, state the path-difference condition for a maximum.

Show answer

Path difference = m lambda, where m is an integer.

22. For in-phase sources, state the path-difference condition for a minimum.

Show answer

Path difference = (m + 1/2) lambda, where m is an integer.

23. What additional condition is required for complete cancellation at a minimum?

Show answer

The two waves must have equal amplitudes at the observation point.

24. Why does a listener hear alternating loud and quiet regions when moving across a two-speaker interference pattern?

Show answer

The path difference changes, changing the arrival phase difference and producing alternating constructive and destructive interference.

25. Two in-phase speakers are equally distant from A. Is A a maximum or a minimum? Explain.

Show answer

A maximum: the path difference is zero, so the waves arrive in phase.

26. Speakers P and Q are 0.30 m apart, with midpoint O. OA = 2.25 m is perpendicular to PQ; AB = 0.95 m is parallel to PQ towards P. Calculate PB.

Show answer

PB = sqrt(2.25^2 + (0.95 - 0.15)^2) = 2.388 m (about 2.39 m).

27. Using the geometry in Question 26, calculate QB.

Show answer

QB = sqrt(2.25^2 + (0.95 + 0.15)^2) = 2.504 m (about 2.50 m).

28. Use the unrounded distances from Questions 26 and 27 to calculate the path difference at B.

Show answer

QB - PB = 0.11651 m, approximately 0.117 m. Subtracting the rounded lengths instead gives 0.11 m.

29. If B is the first maximum after the central maximum A, how is its path difference related to wavelength?

Show answer

The path difference at B equals one wavelength.

30. Using the speech's rounded wavelength of 0.11 m and frequency 2960 Hz, calculate the sound speed.

Show answer

v = f lambda = 2960 x 0.11 = 325.6 m/s (326 m/s to three significant figures).

31. At constant wave speed, what happens to wavelength when frequency decreases?

Show answer

Wavelength increases because lambda = v/f.

32. At a fixed listener position, does changing frequency change the geometric path difference?

Show answer

No. The distances stay fixed; the phase difference changes because the wavelength changes.

33. Can decreasing the frequency always be assumed to make the sound louder at a fixed point?

Show answer

No. Loudness depends on the resulting phase difference. It increases if the waves move closer to being in phase, but can decrease otherwise.

34. Describe the two travelling waves needed to form an ideal stationary wave.

Show answer

They have equal frequency, wavelength and amplitude and travel at the same speed in opposite directions along the same line.

35. Define a node and an antinode.

Show answer

A node has zero oscillation amplitude; an antinode has maximum oscillation amplitude.

36. Compare the phase and frequency of particles in adjacent loops of a stationary wave.

Show answer

They oscillate in antiphase (pi radians apart) at the same frequency.

37. How many loops occur in the second harmonic of a string fixed at both ends?

Show answer

Two loops, with two antinodes and three nodes including the ends.

38. A 0.55 m interval contains two complete wavelengths. At 250 Hz, calculate the travelling-wave speed.

Show answer

lambda = 0.55/2 = 0.275 m; v = 250 x 0.275 = 68.75 m/s (69 m/s to two significant figures).

39. At one instant, two component waves have equal and opposite displacements everywhere. What is the resultant string shape, and do its antinodes disappear?

Show answer

The string is momentarily straight at equilibrium. Antinode positions remain; they are defined by maximum oscillation amplitude over time.

40. A string initially resonates at its fundamental frequency. Its mass per unit length is multiplied by nine while length, tension and driving frequency remain unchanged. Which harmonic is now excited?

Show answer

The third harmonic. Since f1 = (1/(2L)) sqrt(T/mu), the new fundamental is one-third of the old value; the unchanged driving frequency is three times the new fundamental.
