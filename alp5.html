84B. Stationary Waves

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

Formation of Stationary Waves
  • A stationary wave, also called a standing wave, forms when two progressive waves superpose while travelling in opposite directions.
  • The two progressive waves must have the same frequency; in the ideal case they also have equal amplitude.
  • On a rope, both waves oscillate perpendicular to the undisturbed rope while propagating in opposite directions.
  • The resulting pattern remains in a fixed position instead of travelling along the medium.
Stationary and Progressive Waves
  • A progressive wave moves through space as it oscillates.
  • In a stationary wave, peaks and troughs do not propagate along the medium.
  • A fixed point on the stationary wave changes displacement with time.
  • At the same position, a peak can become a trough after half an oscillation.
Superposition and Resultant Displacement
  • The resultant displacement is the vector sum of the displacements of the two progressive waves.
  • When the waves are in phase, their displacements act in the same direction and add together.
  • In-phase alignment produces maximum constructive interference.
  • If two equal-amplitude waves are in antiphase, opposite displacements cancel and the resultant displacement is zero.
Oscillation of a Point
  • A non-node point oscillates between positive and negative displacement.
  • Starting at maximum positive displacement, it reaches zero after one quarter of an oscillation.
  • It reaches maximum negative displacement after another quarter, completing half an oscillation.
  • It then passes through zero and returns to maximum positive displacement to complete one full oscillation.
Nodes
  • Nodes are fixed points where the resultant displacement is always zero.
  • The positions of the nodes do not change as the stationary wave oscillates.
  • Destructive interference occurs continuously at each node.
  • Fixed ends of a taut rope are nodes because they cannot move.
Antinodes
  • Antinodes are points that oscillate with maximum displacement.
  • Each antinode lies midway between two adjacent nodes.
  • An antinode alternates between maximum positive and maximum negative displacement.
  • Nodes should be identified first when marking antinodes on a stationary-wave diagram.
Distances in a Stationary Wave
  • The distance between two adjacent nodes is half a wavelength, λ/2.
  • The distance between a node and its nearest antinode is one quarter of a wavelength, λ/4.
  • One wavelength connects two points at the same stage of their cycles, with the same displacement and velocity.
  • Counting the intervals between nodes helps determine how many half-wavelengths fit into a given length.
Seven-Metre Rope Example
  • A 7 m rope with seven nodes, including both ends, contains six node-to-node intervals.
  • Each interval represents half a wavelength, so six intervals represent three complete wavelengths.
  • Therefore, 3λ = 7 m.
  • The wavelength of each progressive wave is λ = 7/3 = 2.3 m to two significant figures.
