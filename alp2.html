ALPHY2. Phase and Phase Difference

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

0:00 - 1:00 | What Phase Means
  • Phase describes how much of a repeating cycle has been completed.
  • A clock hand is a useful model: the phase can be measured from the 12 o'clock position.
  • For example, a minute hand pointing at 3 has completed 15 minutes out of a 60-minute cycle.
  • The same position can also be described as an angle of 90° or π/2 radians.
1:00 - 2:10 | Measuring Phase as an Angle
  • Measuring phase as an angle lets us compare different repeating motions using the same unit.
  • An hour hand and a minute hand can have the same phase when they point to the same part of the clock, even if they took different amounts of time to get there.
  • In waves, phase is usually measured as an angle because it describes position within a cycle.
2:10 - 3:25 | Phase Difference on Clocks
  • Phase difference is the difference between the phase angles of two repeating motions.
  • If one minute hand has a phase of 90° and another has a phase of 30°, the phase difference is 60°.
  • When two clocks' minute hands move at the same frequency, their phase difference stays constant over time.
  • Phase difference is not constant for motions with different frequencies, such as a clock's hour hand and minute hand.
3:25 - 4:40 | Linking Phase to Waves
  • A wave travels one wavelength during one complete oscillation.
  • One full wave cycle is equivalent to one full revolution around a circle.
  • Therefore, one complete oscillation has a phase of 360° or 2π radians.
  • Half a cycle has a phase of 180° or π radians, and a quarter cycle has a phase of 90° or π/2 radians.
4:40 - 5:45 | Phase and Wavelength Fractions
  • Phase can be described using fractions of a wavelength.
  • A quarter of a wavelength corresponds to a phase of 90°.
  • Half a wavelength corresponds to 180°.
  • One wavelength corresponds to 360° or 2π radians, and two wavelengths correspond to 720° or 4π radians.
5:45 - 7:00 | In Phase, Out of Phase, and Antiphase
  • Two oscillating points are in phase if they reach maximum positive displacement at the same time.
  • They are out of phase if they reach the same stage of the oscillation at different times.
  • They are in antiphase if one point reaches maximum positive displacement when the other reaches maximum negative displacement.
  • Points on the same wave, or different waves with the same frequency, can be compared using these ideas.
7:00 - 8:20 | Comparing Waves with the Same Frequency
  • Phase comparison is most useful when waves have the same frequency.
  • If two waves have the same time period, they also have the same frequency because frequency equals 1 divided by period.
  • At a point of intersection, waves are in phase if they stay at the same stage of oscillation as time passes.
  • Waves are in antiphase if they remain half an oscillation apart.
8:20 - 9:25 | Overlapping Waves in Space
  • When overlapping waves travel in the same direction and are in phase, their peaks, troughs, and zero-displacement points align.
  • Out-of-phase waves do not align perfectly in space.
  • In antiphase, the peaks of one wave align with the troughs of the other wave.
  • This spatial alignment helps identify whether waves are in phase, out of phase, or in antiphase.
9:25 - 10:40 | Calculating Phase Difference
  • The phase difference between two points on the same wave depends on the distance between them.
  • To calculate phase difference in degrees, use: phase difference = (d ÷ λ) × 360°.
  • To calculate phase difference in radians, use: phase difference = (d ÷ λ) × 2π.
  • If two points are separated by one wavelength, their phase difference is 360° or 2π radians.
