ALPHY9. Diffraction Gratings and Grating Spacing

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

What Is a Diffraction Grating?
  • A diffraction grating is a plate with thousands of very narrow, parallel, equally spaced slits. Individual slits are too small to see clearly with the naked eye.
  • Monochromatic light has a single frequency. In the arrangement described, it strikes the grating normally: perpendicular to the grating surface.
Diffraction and Interference
  • Light diffracts at every slit, spreading out so that waves from different slits overlap and interfere.
  • Waves reinforce in certain directions to form bright principal beams. Between these beams, destructive interference produces dark regions; weaker secondary maxima can also occur.
  • The principle is similar to the double-slit experiment, but a grating uses many slits. A screen reveals bright fringes separated by dark regions.
Orders of the Bright Beams
  • The beam along the central axis is the zero-order beam.
  • Moving outwards on either side, the bright principal beams are called first order, second order, and so on.
Grating Spacing
  • Grating spacing, d, is the distance between neighbouring slits.
  • A grating is often specified by its line density: the number of lines per unit length.
  • For N lines in a length L, d = L / N. Equivalently, spacing is the reciprocal of line density when consistent units are used.
  • Use L in metres to obtain d in metres. More lines per unit length means smaller spacing.
Worked Example: 600 Lines per Millimetre
  • The grating is used as a spectrometer to analyse light from a filament bulb.
  • Choose L = 1 mm = 1 x 10^-3 m, containing N = 600 lines.
  • d = (1 x 10^-3) / 600 = 1.666... x 10^-6 m.
  • To three significant figures, d = 1.67 x 10^-6 m = 1.67 micrometres.
  • Correction to the speech: the final exponent should be -6, not -3.
