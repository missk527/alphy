ALP1. Amplitude and Wavelength

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

0:00 - 1:12 | Amplitude and Displacement
  • Waves are made from oscillations that move points away from their equilibrium position.
  • Displacement is a vector, so it must include both distance and direction from equilibrium.
  • One direction can be defined as positive and the opposite direction as negative.
  • Displacement can be shown on a graph against either distance or time.
1:12 - 2:47 | Finding Amplitude
  • Amplitude is the magnitude of the maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.
  • Because amplitude is a magnitude, it is positive even when measured from a negative displacement.
  • The highest points on a wave are called peaks, and the lowest points are called troughs.
  • Amplitude can be measured from the equilibrium line to either a peak or a trough.
2:47 - 5:21 | Wavelength and Wave Cycles
  • Wavelength is the length of one complete oscillation of a wave.
  • It is the distance between adjacent points with the same displacement and velocity.
  • The wavelength symbol is the Greek letter lambda, λ.
  • The simplest way to measure wavelength is between two neighbouring peaks or two neighbouring troughs.
5:21 - 7:04 | Labeling Waves on Graphs
  • On a displacement-distance graph, displacement is placed on the y-axis and distance on the x-axis.
  • Peaks show maximum displacement, while troughs show minimum displacement.
  • Amplitude is the maximum magnitude of displacement from equilibrium.
  • Wavelength can be labeled between two adjacent peaks, two adjacent troughs, or equivalent points in adjacent cycles.
7:04 - 9:10 | Worked Example
  • First label the equilibrium position and the peaks of the wave.
  • Amplitude is found by measuring from the equilibrium position to a peak.
  • In the example, the peak is at 4 m and equilibrium is at 2 m, so the amplitude is 2 m.
  • The wavelength is found from the distance between adjacent peaks: 70 m - 30 m = 40 m.
